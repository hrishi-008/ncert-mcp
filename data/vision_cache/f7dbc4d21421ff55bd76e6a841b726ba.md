If both numbers are doubled, then both numbers get an extra factor of 2 in their prime factors. This 2 will be included as a factor in the largest common subpart, and so the HCF will double. For example, consider the numbers 270 and 50.

[Figure: Prime factorisations with common factors boxed. $270 = 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5$ and $50 = 2 \times 5 \times 5$; the common factors 2 and 5 are boxed in each row.]

$$\text{HCF} = 2 \times 5 = 10$$

Let us double these numbers to get 540 and 100.

[Figure: Prime factorisations with common factors boxed. $540 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5$ and $100 = 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5$; the common factors 2, 2 and 5 are boxed in each row.]

$$\text{HCF} = 2 \times 2 \times 5 = 20.$$

**?** Consider the following two multiples of 14— $14 \times 6$, $14 \times 9$. What is their HCF?

Clearly, 14 is a common factor. Is it also the highest common factor? To see it, let us calculate the prime factorisations.

[Figure: Prime factorisations with common factors boxed. $14 \times 6 = 2 \times 7 \times 2 \times 3$ and $14 \times 9 = 2 \times 7 \times 3 \times 3$; the $2 \times 7$ in each row is marked with a brace as 14, and the common factors $2 \times 7$ together with one 3 are enclosed in a box.]

$$\text{HCF} = 14 \times 3 = 42.$$
