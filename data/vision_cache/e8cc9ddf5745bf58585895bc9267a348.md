> When a pair of lines are not parallel to each other, the corresponding angles formed by a transversal can never be equal to each other.

## 5.7 Drawing Parallel Lines

Can you draw a pair of parallel lines using a ruler and a set square?

Fig. 5.21 shows how you can do it.

Draw a line $l$ with a scale. By sliding your set square you can make two lines perpendicular to line $l$.

Are these two lines parallel to each other? How are we sure that they are parallel to each other? What angles are formed between these lines and line $l$?

[Figure: Fig. 5.21 – A ruler placed along a slanted line $l$, with a set square resting against it; two parallel lines, marked with arrows, are drawn perpendicular to $l$ along the edge of the set square.]

Fig. 5.21

Since we used a set square, the angles measure $90°$. The position of the lines is different, but they make the same angle with $l$. If line $l$ is seen as a transversal to the two new lines, then the corresponding angles measure $90°$.

[Figure: Fig. 5.22 – A ruler placed along line $l$ with a set square against it, its long side used to draw two parallel lines, marked with arrows, slanting up to the right and crossing line $l$.]

Fig. 5.22

As we know these are corresponding angles and they are equal, we can be sure that the lines are parallel.
