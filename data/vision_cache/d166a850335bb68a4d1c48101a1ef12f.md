$$\frac{\overset{1}{\cancel{12}} \times 5}{7 \times \underset{2}{\cancel{24}}} = \frac{1 \times 5}{7 \times 2} = \frac{5}{14}.$$

[Figure: Worked cancellation of $\frac{12 \times 5}{7 \times 24}$, with 12 crossed out and replaced by 1, and 24 crossed out and replaced by 2.]

Let us use the same technique to do one more multiplication.

$$\frac{14}{15} \times \frac{25}{42}$$

$$\frac{\overset{1}{\cancel{14}} \times \overset{5}{\cancel{25}}}{\underset{3}{\cancel{15}} \times \underset{3}{\cancel{42}}} = \frac{1 \times 5}{3 \times 3} = \frac{5}{9}.$$

[Figure: Worked cancellation of $\frac{14}{15} \times \frac{25}{42}$. 14 and 42 are circled in black and crossed out, giving 1 above 14 and 3 below 42. 25 and 15 are circled in red and crossed out, giving 5 above 25 and 3 below 15.]

When multiplying fractions, we can first divide the numerator and denominator by their common factors before multiplying the numerators and denominators. This is called cancelling the common factors.

### A Pinch of History

In India, the process of reducing a fraction to its lowest terms — known as *apavartana* — is so well known that it finds mention even in a non-mathematical work. A Jaina scholar Umasvati (c. 150 CE) used it as a simile in a philosophical work.
