Covering a region using a set of shapes, without gaps or overlaps, is called **tiling**.

Consider a rectangular grid made of unit squares—

[Figure: A rectangular grid of unit squares, all hatched with diagonal lines, with 4 rows and 6 columns.]

We call this a $4 \times 6$ grid, since it has 4 rows and 6 columns.

### Math Talk

Can a $4 \times 6$ grid be tiled using multiple copies of $2 \times 1$ tiles? We are allowed to rotate a $2 \times 1$ tile and use it.

[Figure: Two hatched tiles. A vertical tile made of two unit squares stacked one above the other, labelled "Vertical tile", and a horizontal tile made of two unit squares side by side, labelled "Horizontal tile".]

Here is one way.

[Figure: A $4 \times 6$ grid with a tiling by $2 \times 1$ tiles. Thick lines mark the tile boundaries and green lines through the tiles mark each tile's two squares. The first two columns and the last two columns have horizontal tiles in every row. The middle two columns have vertical tiles, stacked in two pairs of rows.]

Obviously, this is not the only tiling possible.

### Math Talk

Can a $4 \times 7$ grid be tiled using $2 \times 1$ tiles?

What about a $5 \times 7$ grid?

To see that there is no way to tile a $5 \times 7$ grid using $2 \times 1$ tiles, observe that this grid has 35 unit squares. Each tile covers exactly 2 unit squares.
